Toxic plants to recognize first

These are the deck entries Forage Berkeley marks as do not eat. Learn them as recognition cards before you practice edible plants.

Do not eat / Native / Shrub

Poison Oak

Toxicodendron diversilobum

Season
Year-round; red in fall, bare canes in winter
How to recognize it
'Leaves of three,' glossy and scalloped, often reddish; grows as a shrub or climbing vine.
Why it matters
Causes a fierce urushiol rash. Even leafless winter stems trigger it — when in doubt, don't touch.
Recognition boundary
This guide is for recognition practice only, not field use. Confirm real plants with a qualified local source before touching or using them.

Do not eat / Introduced / Herb

Poison Hemlock

Conium maculatum

Season
Spring–summer
How to recognize it
Smooth hairless stems with purple/maroon blotches, fern-like leaves, white flower umbels, musty mousy smell.
Why it matters
DEADLY. Mistaken for fennel or wild carrot — the purple-blotched stems and lack of anise scent give it away. All parts are lethal.
Recognition boundary
This guide is for recognition practice only, not field use. Confirm real plants with a qualified local source before touching or using them.

Sacred Datura (Jimsonweed)

Datura wrightii

Season
Summer–fall
How to recognize it
Sprawling gray-green plant, large soft leaves, huge white trumpet flowers (often opening at dusk), spiny round seed pods.
Why it matters
DEADLY toxic and powerfully hallucinogenic — every part. No safe dose. Do not handle and ingest.

Season
Flowers spring–fall
How to recognize it
Large evergreen shrub, long leathery lance leaves in whorls of three, clusters of pink/white/red flowers.

DEADLY. Among the most poisonous common landscaping plants — never chew leaves or use stems as skewers. Smoke from burning it is toxic too.

Season
Year-round; seeds late summer–fall
How to recognize it
Tall fast-growing weedy shrub, large glossy star-shaped leaves often red-tinged, spiky red-green seed capsules with mottled bean-like seeds.
Why it matters
Extremely poisonous — the seeds especially. Never ingest any part. Included only so you can recognize and avoid it.

Do not eat / Introduced / Tree

English Yew

Taxus baccata

Season
Red 'berries' fall
How to recognize it
Dense evergreen hedge/tree, flat dark-green needles, peeling red-brown bark, soft red cup-shaped 'berries' (arils) with a hard seed.
Why it matters
DEADLY. Needles, bark, and the seed inside the red aril are lethally toxic (taxine alkaloids) — a few chewed seeds or a handful of needles can kill, with no antidote. The only non-toxic part is the thin red flesh, but the deadly seed sits right inside it, so treat the whole plant as off-limits. A very common clipped hedge — never nibble.
Recognition boundary
This guide is for recognition practice only, not field use. Confirm real plants with a qualified local source before touching or using them.

Do not eat / Introduced / Shrub

Privet

Ligustrum lucidum

Season
Flowers summer; black berries fall–winter
How to recognize it
Ubiquitous hedge shrub/tree, glossy pointed opposite leaves, plumes of strong-smelling cream flowers, clusters of small blue-black berries.
Why it matters
Berries and leaves are toxic to people. Among the most common Bay Area hedges — learn it so you never mistake the berries for something edible.

Do not eat / Introduced / Shrub

Firethorn (Pyracantha)

Pyracantha coccinea

Season
Orange-red berries fall–winter
How to recognize it
Dense thorny evergreen hedge, small glossy leaves, masses of tiny white flowers, then sheets of orange-red berry-like pomes.
Why it matters
The seeds contain cyanogenic compounds and the raw pomes are not a safe nibble; the thorns are vicious. Recognition, not food.
Recognition boundary

Do not eat / Introduced / Tree

Horse Chestnut

Aesculus hippocastanum

Season
Candles of flowers spring; conkers fall
How to recognize it
Big tree, large palmate leaves of 5–7 leaflets, upright 'candles' of white flowers, spiny green husks holding glossy brown 'conkers'.
Why it matters
Toxic — the shiny conkers look like edible sweet chestnuts but are NOT (no flattened side, husk is spiny not bristly). Never eat them.
Recognition boundary
This guide is for recognition practice only, not field use. Confirm real plants with a qualified local source before touching or using them.

Common caution and look-alike traps

These plants are not a green light to harvest. They are here because the wrong plant part, preparation, or look-alike can turn a casual nibble into a real risk.

Edible entry / Introduced / Herb

Wild Fennel

Foeniculum vulgare

Season
Fronds spring–fall; seeds late summer
How to recognize it
Feathery thread-like leaves, solid green stems, yellow flower umbels, strong anise/licorice smell when crushed.
Why it matters
Included because it can be confused with deadly poison hemlock. Use the scent and stem clues as a classroom check, not a field decision.
Recognition boundary
This guide is for recognition practice only, not field use. Confirm real plants with a qualified local source before touching or using them.

Blue Elderberry

Season
Flowers late spring; berries summer
How to recognize it
Large shrub/small tree, compound leaves, flat-topped clusters of tiny cream flowers, then dusty-blue berry clusters.
Why it matters
Included because some plant parts and unripe berries can be harmful. Treat it as a plant to confirm with a qualified local source before any real-world use.

Safety boundary

What this guide can and cannot do

Can I use this to decide what to eat?

No. Use it for recognition practice only. Confirm any real plant with a qualified local source before touching, harvesting, or eating it.

Why include edible look-alikes?

Some edible or caution entries are useful because learners confuse them with toxic plants. Wild fennel and poison hemlock are the clearest example.

Is this affiliated with UC Berkeley?

No. Forage Berkeley is an independent learning tool for people around Berkeley and the East Bay.
