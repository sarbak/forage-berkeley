Sidewalk, lawn, and hard-packed edges

Common plants learners tend to notice in paths, parking strips, lawns, and disturbed ground.

Edible · Herb · Year-round; best young in spring

Dandelion

Taraxacum officinale

How to recognize it
Basal rosette of jagged 'lion's tooth' leaves, single hollow stem with milky sap, yellow composite flower, puffball seed head.
Watch for
Avoid sprayed lawns and roadsides when studying real plants. The deck notes that several yellow composite flowers can look similar.
Deck note
The same cosmopolitan weed grows in sidewalk cracks on five continents.

Edible · Herb · Spring–fall

Common Mallow (Cheeseweed)

Malva neglecta

How to recognize it
Round scalloped leaves with a crinkled edge, small pale 5-petal flowers, flat 'cheese-wheel' seed pods.
Watch for
Avoid plants from contaminated soil. This guide treats mallow as recognition practice, not eating clearance.
Deck note
Kids have eaten the 'little cheeses' for centuries.
Open species page

Edible · Herb · Spring–fall

Broadleaf Plantain

Plantago major

How to recognize it
Basal rosette of broad oval leaves with prominent parallel veins; tall slender seed spikes.
Watch for
Older leaves get tough. The deck does not flag a local toxic look-alike, but real plants still need independent confirmation.
Deck note
Followed European settlers so closely it was called 'white man's footprint'.
Open species page

Edible · Herb · Year-round

Common Sow Thistle

Sonchus oleraceus

How to recognize it
Soft (not truly spiny) jagged leaves clasping a hollow stem, milky sap, small dandelion-like yellow flowers.
Watch for
Edges prickle but aren't dangerous; just a milder relative of dandelion.
Deck note
A weed so common most people walk past their lunch.
Open species page

Edible · Herb · Spring–summer

Pineapple Weed

Matricaria discoidea

How to recognize it
Feathery leaves; cone-shaped greenish-yellow flower heads with no petals; pineapple scent when crushed.
Watch for
Confirm the pineapple smell. A petal-less chamomile relative.
Deck note
Thrives in the hard-packed dirt of paths, parking strips, and sidewalk cracks.

6 deck entries

Cool-season garden weeds

Fast-growing herbs that show up in beds, lots, and spring growth around Berkeley.

Edible · Herb · Cool season; fall through spring

Chickweed

Stellaria media

How to recognize it
Low sprawling mats, small opposite egg-shaped leaves, tiny white star flowers, single line of hairs running along the stem.
Watch for
Confirm the single hairline and star flower to avoid spurges (which have milky sap and are toxic).
Deck note
A tender winter green that fills Berkeley garden beds when little else grows.
Open species page

Edible · Herb · Warm season; summer

Common Purslane

Portulaca oleracea

How to recognize it
Fleshy paddle-shaped leaves, smooth reddish stems, low spreading mat, tiny yellow flowers.
Watch for
Distinguish from toxic prostrate spurge, which has thin leaves and milky sap; purslane has thick juicy leaves and no milky sap.
Deck note
A weed in every Bay Area sidewalk crack that's a prized vegetable elsewhere.
Open species page

Edible · Herb · Spring–summer

Lamb's Quarters

Chenopodium album

How to recognize it
Diamond/goosefoot-shaped leaves with a white mealy coating on new growth, upright stem often striped pink.
Watch for
Contains oxalates; cook large servings. Don't confuse with toxic nightshade seedlings.
Deck note
One of the most nutritious wild greens, dismissed as a garden weed.
Open species page

Edible · Herb · Cool season

Shepherd's Purse

Capsella bursa-pastoris

How to recognize it
Basal rosette of toothed leaves; thin stalk of distinctive heart/purse-shaped seed pods.
Watch for
The purse-shaped pods are the reliable ID. Best before flowering.
Deck note
Named for the little coin-purse seed pods lining its stalk.
Open species page

Edible · Herb · Greens late winter; flowers spring

Black Mustard

Brassica nigra

How to recognize it
Tall plant with lobed lower leaves, four-petal yellow flowers, slender upright seed pods.
Watch for
Highly invasive in the deck; learn its four-petal mustard flowers and upright seed pods without treating this page as harvesting advice.
Deck note
The waves of yellow that wash over Bay Area hillsides each spring.
Open species page

Edible · Herb · Spring

Wild Radish

Raphanus sativus

How to recognize it
Lobed rough leaves; four-petal flowers in white/pink/pale yellow; constricted 'beaded' seed pods.
Watch for
None significant; a feral relative of the garden radish.
Deck note
Escaped gardens generations ago and now colors every spring field.

Moist shade and trail-edge plants

Plants from the deck that need extra attention because habitat, stings, or toxic look-alikes can change the learning context.

Edible · Herb · Late winter–spring

Miner's Lettuce

Claytonia perfoliata

How to recognize it
Round disc-like leaf with the flower stem passing straight through its center; tiny white flowers.
Watch for
The perfoliate disc leaf is unmistakable; few problem look-alikes.
Deck note
Gold Rush miners ate it for vitamin C, and the name stuck.
Open species page

Eat with care · Herb · Spring

Stinging Nettle

Urtica dioica

How to recognize it
Opposite toothed leaves, square stems, fine stinging hairs along stems and leaf edges.
Watch for
Stings when raw; use gloves if handling a real plant. The deck notes that heat or drying neutralizes the sting.
Deck note
Hides in damp shady East Bay canyons, waiting for bare ankles.
Open species page

Edible · Herb · Late winter–spring

Three-cornered Leek

Allium triquetrum

How to recognize it
Distinctly three-sided (triangular) flower stem, drooping white bell flowers with a green stripe, strong onion/garlic smell.
Watch for
The deck's allium rule is strict: a true onion or garlic smell matters. No smell means do not treat the plant as an allium.
Deck note
An invasive that conveniently tastes like a free leek.
Open species page

Edible · Herb · Fronds spring–fall; seeds late summer

Wild Fennel

Foeniculum vulgare

How to recognize it
Feathery thread-like leaves, solid green stems, yellow flower umbels, strong anise/licorice smell when crushed.
Watch for
Resembles DEADLY poison hemlock — but hemlock has purple-blotched stems and a musty smell, never anise. If it doesn't smell of licorice, walk away.
Deck note
A Mediterranean escapee that lines East Bay trailheads and BART tracks.
